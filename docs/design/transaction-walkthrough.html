A Harvest Transaction, Step by Step

Alice sells hot sauce. Bob buys a $50 bottle. At each step: what Bob sees, what Alice sees, and what the system is doing underneath — and why.

Status, 9 September 2026

Rewritten for on-chain Bitcoin. An earlier version of this document was built around Lightning throughout, including a clipboard flow for passing a payment secret back; that is gone, and the aside below says why the choice went the other way.

Also updated: a ghostkey is required to file a complaint, not to buy, and store links carry a twelve-character code used directly as the contract's parameters.

Where this disagrees with incentive-mechanism.md, that document wins. Two things described here are not built: the standing and capacity arithmetic, and the confession together with the complaint, cure and window that depend on it.

The one idea underneath everything

Harvest has no operator, no arbitrator, and no way to reverse a payment. Nothing can ever establish who was telling the truth about a delivery — there is no oracle for "did the parcel arrive".

So the design gives up on judging disputes and instead makes them expensive in a way strangers can verify without a judge. A seller posts collateral by burning money; a complaint spends it. Nobody decides who was right. The money moves anyway, and that is what stops fraud paying.

Before any of this: Alice sets up shop

0 Alice builds her bond

Bob

Not involved — he has never heard of Alice yet.

Alice

Donates to Freenet to create an anonymous identity, and donates more later as her shop grows. She sees her position in Harvest:

Your bond: $500 · You can carry up to $250 of orders at once

To take bigger orders, she donates more. That is the whole interaction.

Underneath

Each donation produces a certificate from the issuer binding the amount to Alice's identity key. The certificates sit in her public reputation record, where anyone can verify them against Freenet's master key. The money is gone — donated, not held in escrow.

Why burned rather than held

Escrow needs somebody to hold the money, and there is nobody. Burning is the only way to make a cost verifiable by strangers with no custodian — and it cannot be quietly withdrawn the night before a scam.

Why it grows instead of being a fixed tier

With a fixed tier, a brand-new identity and a five-year-old spotless one look identical, so the worst that can happen to a cheat is paying the entry fee again. A growing bond restores the thing that makes reputation work at all: history has value, and walking away costs what it cost to build.

The transaction

1 Bob decides whether Alice is good for it

Bob arrived by a link ending in #store= and twelve characters — a base58 prefix of Alice's verifying key, used directly as the contract's parameters. His client derives the contract key locally from that prefix plus the public contract code, so there is no lookup, no directory, and no index of stores anywhere. Twelve rather than ten characters is a deliberate margin against a seller grinding a key that collides with someone else's prefix.

Bob

Browsing a listing, he sees a line beneath the price:

Seller has $500 staked · $60 of orders in progress · your $50 order is well covered

He clicks buy. Nothing else is asked of him.

Alice

Sees nothing. She does not know Bob is looking.

Underneath

Bob's software fetches Alice's reputation record, checks her certificates chain back to Freenet's master key, adds up her donations, then subtracts twice any unrefunded complaints and twice her currently open orders. $500 − $120 = $380 of spare capacity; his $50 order needs $100 of it.

What Bob is really asking

Not "does this person seem honest" but "is more money staked here than I am about to risk?" — a question with an arithmetic answer, which is the point. It requires trusting nobody's judgement, including Bob's own.

Why Bob's own software does the sum

The figure goes down when complaints land, so it is not stable. A Freenet contract must reach the same verdict on the same data forever or peers permanently disagree, so no contract may depend on a value that moves. Bob's own software has no such obligation, so it can freely count, weigh and expire things. The network stores facts; the reader forms the verdict.

2 Alice publicly declares the order

Bob Waiting for the seller to confirm…
Alice

An order appears. She clicks accept.

Bob wants 1 × Ghost Pepper, $50 — Accept? This will use $100 of your $380 spare capacity.
Underneath

Accepting writes an order commitment into Alice's public record: a scrambled order number, the amount, and a recent Bitcoin block hash. It reveals nothing about who Bob is or what he bought.

Why this exists at all — it is the anti-scam mechanism

It makes Alice's total exposure countable by strangers. Without it, orders are private, so nobody can see how much she currently owes in undelivered goods.

Why it must happen before payment

If Alice could take money without declaring it, she could quietly run $5,000 of orders against $500 of collateral and vanish $4,500 ahead. Declaring first caps what she can ever be holding at once: she cannot take money without first admitting, in public, that she owes goods.

Why a Bitcoin block hash

Contracts cannot read a clock. Embedding a recent block hash proves the entry is no earlier than that block, and the reader's own clock says how long ago that was. The asymmetry falls the right way: you cannot make a fresh order look old, because looking old requires having published early — which is exactly the behaviour being forced.

3 Bob's software checks the declaration is real

Bob Confirming… ✓ Seller confirmed. Ready to pay.

Takes a few seconds. He does nothing.

Alice

Nothing to do.

Underneath

Bob's software reads Alice's record back from the network, confirms his own order is actually in there, and re-checks that her total open orders still fit inside her bond.

Two jobs in one rule

Requiring Bob to see his own entry is what forces Alice to publish at all — an undeclared order collects no money. Re-checking the total is what stops Bob being the order that tips her past her collateral.

Why the buyer enforces this rather than the network

Because it is selfish. Bob is not policing the marketplace, he is protecting himself — which is what makes the rule hold without anyone enforcing it. Every buyer follows it for their own sake, and the cap is the collective side effect.

4 Alice sends a bill and a pre-signed confession

Bob Pay $50 [Open wallet] — If this never arrives, you'll be able to claim against the seller's bond.
Alice

Nothing by hand. Her delegate derives a fresh Bitcoin address for this order alone, from her payment xpub, offline.

Address issued ✓ Amount and order matched.
Underneath

Alice's software signs a confession — a statement in her own name reading "I failed to deliver order N, amount $50" — and sends it privately to Bob along with the address. The order id is a hash over the whole order including the payment script and the amount, so the confession is bound to that address and that amount for free.

Why the seller signs a confession in advance

There is no oracle for delivery and never will be, so no third party can ever attest that Alice failed. The only unforgeable evidence available is a statement Alice wrote herself, beforehand. She cannot dispute it later, because it carries her signature.

Why it takes two halves

The confession is useless on its own. It only becomes usable together with proof that this buyer actually paid — so the ability to damage Alice costs exactly $50.

Why the confession is not simply part of the public commitment

Both are Alice-signed statements about the same order, made at the same moment, and she signs a confession on every order rather than only ones she expects to fail. So they look like one thing split in two, and the reason they are not is specific to paying on chain: proof of payment is public. Anyone watching the chain sees the payment land. So the confession is the only half that can be private, and folding it into the public commitment would make complaints free for strangers again. Lightning would have supplied the private half from the payment rail itself; on chain it has to be this message.

Without that tie

Complaints would be free. Anyone could open a hundred conversations, collect a hundred signed confessions, pay for none, and file them all — destroying any seller at no cost. That was the fatal flaw in the earlier version of this design.

An aside: how any of this reaches Bitcoin

The obvious objection is that Freenet cannot talk to a payment network. That is correct, and more absolutely than you might expect — but it turns out not to matter. The steps resume afterwards.

Nothing inside Freenet can make a network call

Contracts and delegates have no network capability whatsoever — the runtime grants six host functions covering storage, secrets, logging, randomness and time, and nothing else. A delegate could hold a wallet key and sign a transaction, but could never broadcast it.

The web interface cannot either. The gateway runs it in a sandbox permitted to contact only its own origin — so no request to an outside payment service, not even one on the same machine.

This is why the rail is on-chain, not Lightning

A Lightning invoice has to be minted by a node at the moment of sale, and minting is a network call. So Lightning would have required the seller, or some service acting for them, to be reachable and responsive every time anyone places an order, permanently.

An on-chain address is different: it can be derived offline, from a key the delegate already holds, with no network and nobody awake. That is what keeps automatic, delegate-side acceptance of orders reachable at all. Foreclosing it was the decisive argument against Lightning, not a detail.

And the protocol still never needs a network call

Alice publishes a commitment: pure Freenet. Her delegate derives the address: local computation. She signs a confession: pure Freenet. Bob pays: his wallet, outside. Alice learns she has been paid by looking at her own wallet, exactly as any merchant with a payment page does. Nothing inside Freenet has to reach out and detect a settlement.

What crosses the boundary is a bridge: a party that watches Bitcoin and publishes signed observations onto the network, where a contract reads them as ordinary data.

What the bridge is trusted forWhat it cannot do
Chain state: which blocks exist, their heights, where the tip is. Nothing verifies this against Bitcoin itself, so a holder of a trusted bridge key could settle an order nobody paid.Misreport what a real transaction paid, or to whom. Each claim is checked against the transaction its txid commits to and is bound to this order's own script and network.
This is not a new trusted party — the transition to Paid already rests on it, so a successful purchase depends on the bridge exactly as a complaint does.Repoint somebody else's payment at this order.

The trusted-bridge list is therefore part of what the seller signs, per order, and the interface flags bridges the build does not recognise.

Back to the transaction

5 Bob pays

Bob

Taps the pay button, his wallet opens, he confirms. If his wallet is connected, that is the end of it; otherwise he pastes a short code back.

Paid ✓ You can claim against this order if it doesn't arrive.
Alice

Sees $50 arrive in her wallet — not in Harvest. Harvest does not know yet.

Underneath

Bob pays the address from any wallet. A bridge publishes signed claims about that address, and the order's move to Paid is authorised by that evidence and signed by nobody — any peer can verify it, so any peer can submit it, and Bob's own client normally does. Bob now holds a complete, usable complaint: Alice's confession, plus a payment he can point to. He could not have obtained the second half without paying.

6 Alice ships

Bob Marked as shipped by seller
Alice

Posts the bottle. Optionally marks it shipped.

Underneath

Nothing. Shipping is invisible to the system, which is the whole reason the rest of the design is shaped as it is.

Then one of three things

7a It arrives — and nothing happens at all

Bob

Enjoys the hot sauce. Does nothing.

Alice

After a while her capacity quietly returns.

Spare capacity: $380 → $480
Underneath

No writes of any kind. The order commitment ages past the complaint window and readers begin counting it closed.

Why this matters

Silence is success. The overwhelmingly common outcome records nothing, which keeps the whole system small — a busy seller's record only grows when things go wrong.

Why "closed" is a judgement, not a deletion

Contracts cannot expire anything and records are append-only, so nothing is ever removed. "Old enough to ignore" is a conclusion each reader draws for itself from the block anchor — the same principle as step 1.

7b It never arrives — Bob complains

Bob This order is overdue. File a claim? This reduces the seller's bond by $100 and is public — your buyer identity will be attached to it.

He clicks file.

Alice A claim was filed against order N. Your bond: $500 → $400. Refund $50 to clear it.
Underneath

Bob presents a ghostkey — the only point in the whole flow where a buyer needs one, since buying is anonymous and free — and publishes Alice's own signed confession together with the proof of payment. The network checks her signature, checks the proof against the order's address and amount, and checks the amounts agree — all pure arithmetic, no clock, nothing fetched from elsewhere. Every reader now computes $500 − (2 × $50) = $400.

Why a ghostkey to complain, but not to buy

A ghostkey costs a donation, and that cost is what limits repeated extortion: the history accrues to it across sellers. Requiring one to buy would charge every honest buyer for a problem only a dishonest one creates.

Why a $50 theft costs $100 of bond

If a complaint cost exactly what was stolen, the crime would fund itself — steal $50, lose $50, top up with the stolen $50, break even. The multiplier must exceed one, and in fact must exceed one divided by the share of victims who actually bother to complain. If only half do, it needs to be above two.

Why this counts as a fine when nobody collects it

Nobody takes anything — the money was burned long ago. What makes it bite is simply that every buyer's software agrees to subtract it. That is a convention rather than a law, but it holds because each buyer applies it to protect themselves, not out of public spirit.

7c Alice made a mistake and fixes it

Bob

Gets his $50 back.

Refunded ✓ Claim marked resolved.
Alice

Sends Bob a refund on chain, and publishes proof of it.

Bond restored: $400 → $500
Underneath

Alice publishes proof of the refund. Readers treat the complaint as cured and stop subtracting it.

Three jobs at once

Honest errors stay survivable — a lost parcel should not permanently cost a seller twice its value. Complaining becomes worth doing — Bob files to get his money back, not merely to punish, so more victims complain, which makes the deterrent stronger for everyone. And it caps extortion: a dishonest buyer who received his goods and demands a refund anyway can extract the order value, not the penalty, because refunding is Alice's cheaper option.

Why even a scammer refunds

Refunding costs $50; taking the hit costs $100 of bond. Anyone who intends to keep selling refunds — which usefully means victims who complain tend to get paid by any seller who is not already leaving.

What the arrangement buys

ProblemHow it is addressed
Seller takes the money and vanishesOpen orders can never exceed the bond, so the maximum haul is always less than the bond destroyed. Unprofitable at any size.
Complaints as free ammunitionA complaint needs both Alice's private confession and proof of payment, so it costs the order value to obtain.
"Two complaints" — out of how many?The question stops mattering. Complaints are withdrawals from a stated balance, not a rate needing a denominator.
Faking a good reputationThe only way to raise a bond is to burn real money. The signal is the cost.
Abandoning a damaged identityPointless — a complained-about identity is not tainted, just poorer, and a fresh one costs what the old one cost.
Growth making sellers more temptableInverted. Larger orders require more collateral posted first, so capacity and exposure rise together.

Three ideas doing all the work

1. The network stores facts; software forms verdicts

Every "a contract cannot do that" obstacle — no clock, cannot count, cannot expire things — applies to what the network validates, not to the software reading it. Keep the record as an append-only pile of self-proving signed facts and move all judgement into the reader, and the obstacles mostly evaporate.

2. Every write must cost real money

Anyone can write to a Freenet contract, so spam prevention cannot rely on permission. Here it does not have to: raising a bond needs a donation, filing a complaint needs a paid order, and a refund proof needs an actual refund. There is no free write anywhere in the design.

3. Disputes get priced, never judged

No mechanism here can work out who was lying — that is a hard limit, not a gap to fill in later. What it can do is ensure every dispute costs both sides real money in proportion to the order, so neither defrauding people nor falsely accusing them works as a business.

What this honestly does not fix

A buyer who receives his goods and demands a refund anyway still gets away with roughly the order value, once. His identity accumulates a visible history of complaints, which limits repetition, but it cannot be driven to zero — that follows from having no way to tell a real victim from a false one.

And a genuine victim of a seller who is deliberately leaving still loses their money. The complaint they file destroys a bond the seller had already written off.

Three more, specific to taking payment on chain. The bridge is trusted for chain state — a holder of a trusted bridge key could settle an order nobody paid, and nothing here checks its word against Bitcoin. Proof of payment is public, so the whole mechanism rests on a confession reaching exactly one buyer privately before they pay; a leaked confession is a complaint anyone watching the chain could file, and Lightning's preimage would have prevented that for free. And an order reveals what was bought, because the commitment names its listing and listings are public — so the public ledger is itemised, not merely valued.
